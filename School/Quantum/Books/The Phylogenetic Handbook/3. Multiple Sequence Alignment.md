## 3.1 Introduction
An alignment is an **homology hypothesis** either abour *proteins* or *nucleotides*. Thus, aligned sequences are assumed to come from a common place.

Not all the alignments have **the same length**, and thus, to align multiple sequences wi have to add hyphens (-). These hyphens represent **deletions**, **insertions** or a **combination of them**.

Alignments are an essential task, and are a **prerequisite** for any phylogenetic analysis.

## 3.2 The Problem of Repeats
A factor that increases alignment difficulty are **repeats**, sections in the same sequence that are similar with each other.

One way to solve this is making a **phylogenetic analysis** of all the repeats in the sequence. But the results are not always *satisfactory*.

## 3.3 The Problem of Substitutions
Substitutions are when nucleotides change, when they are a few there are no problems, but when the **sequence identity** (identical pairs / aligned positions) drops below certain values, it becomes *difficult* to find a *good alignment*.

When analysing sequences, there are a few **substitution patterns** to take into account:
1. Substitutions are not evenly distributed, there are **blocks** with very few substitutions. (This makes it *easier* for algorithms to find alignments, it is due to the *presence of essential regions*).
2. Aligned pairs are **biochemically similar**. The most important property conserved is *polarity/hydrophobicity*.

How biochemically similar two sequences are can be seen using the PAM or BLOSUM matrices as seen in the **section 2.3** of [[1. Similarity Searching Using Blast]]. For instance, the higher the biochemical similarity, the *higher the weight*.

It is important to state that in **nucleotide sequences** is much more difficult to detect if the substitutions are *conservative* or not. That is why if it is possible, we should align *protein coding sequences at the amino acid level*.

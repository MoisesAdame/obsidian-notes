## 2.1 Introduction
Sequence data is stored in **digital databases**. You can search sequences by id, keyword or even similarity.

## 2.2 Sequence Databases
### 2.2.1 General Nucleic Acid Sequence Databases
The main databases are:
- European Molecular Biology Laboratory (EMBL).
- GenBank
- DNA Data Bank of Japan (DDBJ)

They **cross-submit** all their data. By convention, the sequences are of DNA.

Every sequence has the following parameters:
- **Entry name, locus name or id (ID):** The identifier of the sequence.
- **Accession Number (AC):** It is constant across all databases. There is a *primary* and *secondary*.
- **Version Number:** The AC + increment.
- **GeneInfo Number (GeneBank only):** A unique number given by the NCBI (the admins of GeneBank).
- **Whole Genome Shotgun (WGS) sequences:** Sequences from genomes obtained via the *shotgun* method.
- **Third Party Annotations (TPA):** They have extra stuff, submitted by a person who is not the author.

### 2.2.2 General Protein Sequence Databases
Obtained through different methods. Generally have *errors*. There are plenty of paces to store them, but the most important place is **UniProt**. There are tow faces of UniProt:
- **UniProt/SwissProt:** Human made.
- **UniProt/TrEMBL:** Computer generated. Used previously before entering SwissProt.

### 2.2.3 Specialized Sequence Databases, Reference Databases and Genome Databases
They are better because are **faster**, has **less errors**, easier to use and has better **quality**.

## 2.3 Composite Databases, Database Mirroring and Search Tools
### 2.3.1 Entrez
Maintained by the NCBI, stores everything from *books*, *nucleotides*, *3D models*, etc.

### 2.3.2 Sequence Retrieval System
A way of storing *locally* often used databases using *XML* of *ASCII*. There are various servers that do this.

### 2.3.3 Some General Considerations About Database Searching by Keyword
There are many terms and they depend on how the authors submitted them, so it is useful to **repeat the query multiple times**.

Things to take into account while making queries:
- In **ENTREZ** and **SRS** queries are *case insensitive*.
- They allow the use of *wild cards (?, \*)*
- Queries can be composed using *logical operators (&, |, !)*.

## 2.4 Database Searching by Sequence Similarity
### 2.4.1 Optimal Alignment
To see in tow sequences are related we use **alignment**. In every alignment we cal calculate the *similarity score*. There are many ways, depending on the subject (bases, amino acids, proteins). The lower the similarity score, the less quality the alignment has.

Often we use **computational methods** to obtain these alignments, but they are not always true.

We can establish a *threshold* for similarity score and use only acceptable sequences, but this *can take a lot of time*. To do so, we use generally a set of computers.

### 2.4.2 Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST)
This is a *heuristic* method of high accuracy but much less cost (time). There have been many versions that have parameters that trade speed against sensitivity.

The BLAST program has many modules dedicated for bases, proteins, different translations, etc.

### 2.4.3 FASTA
Is an older algorithm than BLAST.

### 2.4.4 Other Tools and Some General Considerations
There are faster methods that use *indexation* like **SSAHA** and **BLAT**.

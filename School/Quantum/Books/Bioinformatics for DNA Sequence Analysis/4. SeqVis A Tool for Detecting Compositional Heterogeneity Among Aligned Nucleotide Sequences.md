## 1. Introduction
### 1.1 Compositional Heterogeneity
**Compositional Heterogeneity** is the property that describes differences in the composition of quantifiable traits within or among *genomes*. For example *nucleotides* or *amino acids*.

### 1.2 Phylogenetic Assumptions
Research suggests that compositional heterogeneity is present among **homologous sequences**. This has implications on the phylogenetic analysis. There are three types of evolution:
- **Stationary:** No changes between new and ancestral sequence.
- **Reversible:** Equal probability of sampling a given nucleotide.
- **Homogeneous:** Rate of change is constant.

Additionally the evolution can be **local** if it only occurs in a single edge, or **global** if it occurs in the whole three.

We still need a model that detects the type of mutation.

### 1.3 Methods of Detecting Violated Phylogenetic Assumptions
These methods have been used to **detect compositional heterogeneity** and are:
1. **Monte Carlo Methods:** Simple, but often limited or biased.
2. **N x M Contingency Table Methods:** They use one or many tests, but in general they have misleading results.
3. **Symmetry Test Methods:** An array that counts the matching pairs. Not widely used, but very appropriate for detecting *violated phylogenetic assumptions*.
4. **Disparity Index Methods:** This index is calculated based on the *frequency of pairs in two sequences* and on the *number of sites where the sites differ*. It i a good method but should be used with caution.
5. **Relative Nucleotide/Amino Acid Frequencies Methods:** First, they calculate an *euclidean distance between sequences*. They are simple, but not that useful.
6. **Bi-Tri-Dimensional Plot Methods:** Plot the nucleotide in two or three dimensions. Good but has some faults.

In short, there are many methods, but due to the problem's nature, more than one has to be used to detect nuances.

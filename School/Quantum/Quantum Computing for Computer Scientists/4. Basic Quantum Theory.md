## 4.1 Quantum States
Through the XIX Century there was a discussion whether if light was solely waves and matter was solely made out of particles. Using experiments such as the **diffraction experiment** and the **photoelectric experiment**, scientists found out that both matter and light have particle and wave-like behavior.

![[fig4.1.png|500]]

Let's say we want to know the probability of a subatomic particle to land on one of the many points on a line where it can land. For instance, each one of them can be expressed as the following:
$$
\begin{matrix}
\ket{x_{1}} \to 
\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 0 & \cdots & 0
\end{bmatrix} \\
\ket{x_{2}} \to 
\begin{bmatrix}
0 & 1 & \cdots & 0
\end{bmatrix} \\
\vdots \\
\ket{x_{n}} \to 
\begin{bmatrix}
0 & 0 & \cdots & 1
\end{bmatrix}
\end{matrix}
$$

As we have seen, initially the particle is in a **fuzzy** state that we don't really know, which is a **superposition** of all its possible states, that can be represented as:
$$
\ket{\psi} = c_{1}\ket{x_{1}} + c_{2}\ket{x_{2}} + \cdots + c_{n}\ket{x_{n}}
$$

We say that each $c_{i}$ is a **complex amplitude**, and this combination is useful for obtaining the probability of the particle of landing in a given state, obtained from:
$$
p(x_{i}) = \frac{\left| c_{i} \right|^{2}}{\left| \ket{\psi}  \right|^{2}} = \frac{\left| c_{i} \right|^{2}}{\sum_{j = 1}^{n}\left| c_{j} \right|^{2}}
$$

### Equal Quantum State
Two **complex states** $\ket{\psi_{1}}$ and $\ket{\psi_{2}}$ are the same if there exists a $c \in \mathbb{C}$ such that $\ket{\psi_{1}} = c\ket{\psi_{2}}$. This is because:
$$
p(x_{i}) = \frac{\left| c_{1i} \right|^{2}}{\left| \ket{\psi_{1}}  \right|^{2}} = \frac{\left| cc_{2i} \right|^{2}}{\left| \ket{c\psi_{2}}  \right|^{2}} = \frac{\left| c \right|^{2} \left| c_{2i} \right|^{2}}{\left| c \right|^{2} \left| \ket{\psi_{2}}  \right|^{2}} = \frac{\left| c_{2i} \right|^{2}}{\left| \ket{\psi_{2}}  \right|^{2}}
$$

### Normalized $\ket{\psi}$
A normalized vector has a **unitary length**. It can be calculated as follows:
$$
\hat{\ket{\psi}} = \frac{\ket{\psi}}{\left| \ket{\psi} \right|}
$$
Because $\left| \ket{\psi} \right| = 1$, then the probability is $p(x_{i}) = \left| c_{i} \right|^{2}$

### The Role of the Spin
The electrons act as a **magnet** charged positively of negatively spinning accordingly. As anything in the quantum field, it is not just positive or negative, but when measured, we find only those properties. So, the way to describe them is the following:
$$
\ket{\psi} = c_{1}\ket{\uparrow} + c_{2}\ket{\downarrow} 
$$
### Inner Products and Bra's
Initially, the inner product was an abstract mathematical process. In quantum computing helps us to calculate the transition amplitude, or how likely the measurement of a state will be, given an initial state, after measuring. 

Let $\ket{\psi}$ and $\ket{\psi'}$ be two normalized states where $\ket{\psi}$ is the **starting** state.
$$
\ket{\psi} = 
\begin{bmatrix}
c_{1} \\
c_{2} \\
\vdots \\
c_{n} \\
\end{bmatrix}, ~ 
\ket{\psi'} =
\begin{bmatrix}
c_{1}' \\
c_{2}' \\
\vdots \\
c_{n}' \\
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Now, the **ending** state of $\ket{\psi'}$ is **bra** it's bra shown as follows:
$$
\bra{\psi'} = \ket{\psi'}^{\dagger} = 
\begin{bmatrix}
\overline{c_{1}'} & \overline{c_{2}'} & \cdots & \overline{c_{n}'}
\end{bmatrix}
$$
Finally we find the inner product between the bra and the ket, thus obtaining the **transition amplitude**:
$$
\bra{\psi'}\ket{\psi} = 
\begin{bmatrix}
\overline{c_{1}'} & \overline{c_{2}'} & \cdots & \overline{c_{n}'}
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
c_{1} \\
c_{2} \\
\vdots \\
c_{n} \\
\end{bmatrix} =
\overline{c_{1}'} c_{1} + \overline{c_{2}'} c_{2} + \cdots + \overline{c_{n}'} c_{n}
$$

Now, imagine that we want to know the probability of a $\ket{\uparrow}$ to end up in $\ket{\downarrow}$. Because their are **orthogonal**, their inner product is 0, thus, making it impossible.

## 4.2 Observables
### Introduction
In a physical system we are surrounded by observables, where each observable is a physical quantity in each state. Each observable can be thought of as a **question to the system**.

> [!NOTE]
> To each physical observable there corresponds a **hermitian operator** (as seen in [[2. Complex Vector Spaces]]).
> 
> The **eigenvalues** of a hermitian operator associated with a physical observable are the only possible values observable can take as a result of measuring it on any given state. Furthermore, the eigenvectors of form a basis for the state space.

![[fig4.1.png|500]]

Consider the initial system for measuring the position of a particle in a horizontal line, where $\{ x_{0}, x_{1}, \dots, x_{n-1} \}$ are the coordinates of the particle and $\{ \ket{x_{0}}, \ket{x_{1}}, \dots, \ket{x_{n-1}} \}$ are the states in each position. Considering the previous note and the fact that $\Omega \vec{v} = \lambda \vec{v}$. Considering all this, we can see that:
$$
\begin{matrix}
\ket{x_{0}} \to x_{0} \\
\ket{x_{1}} \to x_{1} \\
\vdots \\
\ket{x_{n-1}} \to x_{n-1} \\
\end{matrix} ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ 
\begin{matrix}
\Omega\ket{x_{0}} = x_{0}\ket{x_{0}} \\
\Omega\ket{x_{1}} = x_{1}\ket{x_{1}} \\
\vdots \\
\Omega\ket{x_{n-1}} = x_{n-1}\ket{x_{n-1}} \\
\end{matrix}
$$


By this, we can conclude that:
$$
\Omega = 
\begin{bmatrix}
x_{0} & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\
0 & x_{1} & 0 & 0 \\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
0 & 0 & \cdots & x_{n-1}
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Which clearly is an **hermitian linear operator**.

### Observables as a Vector Space
In any physics course we see that the manipulation of observables can give us other observables, for example weight is the multiplication of mass times gravity. The same happens with our **hermitian linear operators**, we can multiply the by a real scalar, and even add them, thus, making a **Real Vector Space**.

There are many downsides, first, **multiplication isn't useful** because it is not commutative as with scalars, second, their **product isn't always hermitian**. In fact, the operators are commutative over multiplication only when the **commutator** is equal to zero, that is:
$$
[\Omega_{1}, \Omega_{2}] = \Omega_{1}\Omega_{2} - \Omega_{2}\Omega_{1} = 0
$$

Additionally, we known that hermitian operators behave well under the **inner product**, that is:
$$
\braket{ \Omega \phi | \psi } = \braket{ \phi | \Omega \psi } 
$$
$$
\braket{ \Omega \psi | \psi } = \braket{ \Omega }_{\psi} \in \mathbb{R}
$$

### Quantum Expected Value
This value is called the **expectation value** (as seen in [[40. Quantum Mechanics]]) and can be thought of as the expected value of the measurement of an experiment. Mathematically, it is the average of all the possible outcomes weighted by their probabilities of occurring, that is:
$$
\braket{ \Omega }_{\psi} \approx \lambda_{0} \frac{p_{0}}{n} + \lambda_{1} \frac{p_{1}}{n} + \cdots + \lambda_{n-1} \frac{p_{n-1}}{n}
$$

Where $n$ is the times the experiment has been made, and $p_{i}$ is the times the result was $\lambda_{i}$.

### Quantum Variance Operator
Sooner or later we may be interested in knowing the spread of the distribution around its expected value. A small **variance** means most eigenvalues are close to the mean, while a large one tells us the opposite. The operator is the following:
$$
\Delta_{\psi}(\Omega) = \Omega - \braket{ \Omega }_{\psi}I 
$$

If we apply this operator to a given state, it would look like this:
$$
\Delta_{\psi}(\Omega)\ket{\phi}  = \Omega(\ket{\phi}) - \braket{ \Omega }_{\psi}\ket{\phi} 
$$

Considering this, we can define the **variance** as follows:
$$
Var_{\psi}(\Omega) = \braket{ \Delta_{\psi}(\Omega) \cdot \Delta_{\psi}(\Omega) }_{\psi}
$$

### Heisenberg’s Uncertainty Principle
#### Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality
Let a superposition of vectors be $\ket{x} = \ket{\alpha} + \lambda\ket{\beta}$. Using this, we know that $\braket{ x | x } \geq 0$. Thus:
$$
(\ket{\alpha} + \lambda\ket{\beta})^{\dagger} (\ket{\alpha} + \lambda\ket{\beta}) \geq 0
$$
$$
(\bra{a} + \lambda^{*}\bra{\beta} )(\ket{\alpha} + \lambda\ket{\beta}) \geq 0
$$
$$
\braket{ \alpha | \alpha } + \lambda \braket{ \alpha | \beta } + \lambda^{*} \braket{ \beta | \alpha } + \left| \lambda \right|^{2}\braket{ \beta | \beta } \geq 0
$$

Of course we don't know the real value of $\lambda$, but we can define it as follows:
$$
\lambda = -\frac{\braket{ \beta | \alpha } }{\braket{ \beta | \beta } }, ~ \lambda^{*} = -\frac{\braket{ \alpha | \beta } }{\braket{ \beta | \beta } } \in \mathbb{C}
$$

Substituting, we see that:
$$
\braket{ \alpha | \alpha } - \frac{|\braket{ \alpha | \beta }|^{2}}{\braket{ \beta | \beta }} - \frac{|\braket{ \alpha | \beta }|^{2}}{\braket{ \beta | \beta }} + \frac{|\braket{ \alpha | \beta }|^{2}}{\braket{ \beta | \beta }} \geq 0
$$

Ending up with:
$$
\braket{ \alpha | \alpha }\braket{ \beta | \beta } \geq \left| \braket{ \alpha | \beta }  \right|^{2}
$$

#### Using the Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality
Consider the states $\ket{\psi_{A}} = \Delta_{\psi}(A) \ket{\psi}$ and $\ket{\psi_{B}} = \Delta_{\psi}(B) \ket{\psi}$, we can see that:
$$
\braket{ \psi_{A} | \psi_{A} } = \bra{\psi} \Delta_{\psi}(A)^{*} \Delta_{\psi}(A) \ket{\psi} = \braket{ \Delta_{\psi}(A)^{2} }_{\psi}
$$
$$
\braket{ \psi_{B} | \psi_{B} } = \bra{\psi} \Delta_{\psi}(B)^{*} \Delta_{\psi}(B) \ket{\psi} = \braket{ \Delta_{\psi}(B)^{2} }_{\psi}
$$
$$
\braket{ \psi_{A} | \psi_{B} } = \bra{\psi} \Delta_{\psi}(A)^{*} \Delta_{\psi}(B) \ket{\psi} = \braket{ \Delta_{\psi}(A) \Delta_{\psi}(B) }_{\psi}
$$

Inserting this, into the *Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality*, we get:
$$
\braket{ \Delta_{\psi}(A)^{2} }_{\psi}\braket{ \Delta_{\psi}(B)^{2} }_{\psi} \geq \left| \braket{ \Delta_{\psi}(A) \Delta_{\psi}(B) }_{\psi} \right|^{2}
$$

Now, considering the following *commutator, anti-commutator relation*:
$$
\frac{1}{2}[X, Y] + \frac{1}{2}\{ X, Y \} = \frac{1}{2}(XY - YX) + \frac{1}{2}(XY + YX) = XY
$$
$$
\braket{ \Delta_{\psi}(A)^{2} }_{\psi}\braket{ \Delta_{\psi}(B)^{2} }_{\psi} \geq \left| \braket{ \frac{1}{2}[ \Delta_{\psi}(A) , \Delta_{\psi}(B)  ] + \frac{1}{2}\{ \Delta_{\psi}(A) , \Delta_{\psi}(B) \} }_{\psi}  \right|^{2}
$$

Substituting and rearranging:
$$
Var_{\psi}(A) \times Var_{\psi}(B) \geq \frac{1}{4} \left| \braket{ [ \Delta_{\psi}(A), \Delta_{\psi}(B) ] }_{\psi} \right|^{2}
$$

Thus getting the general formula for the **Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle**. Thus, the general idea of this principle is that for any pair of observables, the less variance wi have in one, the more variance he have in the other

## 4.3 Measuring
### Main Idea
In quantum physics it is well known that **systems get perturbed after measuring them**. Also, we can calculate the **probability** of getting certain result, thus, making measurement a **non-deterministic** process.

Now, consider an observable $\Omega$ and an state $\ket{\psi}$. If the result of measuring $\Omega$ is the eigenvalue $\lambda_{i}$, then the state after measurement the eigenvector corresponding to $\lambda_{i}$.

If we want to obtain the probability of a normalized state $\ket{\psi}$ to collapse into an eigenvector $\ket{e}$, we just have to:
$$
P(\ket{\psi} \to \ket{e}) = \left| \braket{ e | \psi }  \right|^{2}
$$

### Measuring More Than One Observable
Assume that we have more than one observable (each of which has a different set of eigenvectors). Then, the resulting measurement will depend on the **order** in which we perform the measurements.

Consider a set of polarized lenses that are one in front of another. If both are in the same direction, it will be like **measuring two times**, thus, there will be no change in the light we se if we only used one. Conversely, if we rotate one 90° with respect of the other, the photons that didn't collapse in the fist one will in the second one, leaving us with nothing to see.

## 4.4 Dynamics
### Introduction
We've only studied quantum systems that **do not evolve over time**, in reality, systems change, thus we need to consider **quantum dynamics**.

One thing to notice is that the way **hermitian operators** are related to physical operators, **unitary operators** are related to quantum dynamics.

For instance, if $U$ is a **unitary operator** and $\ket{\psi(t)}$ is a state at a time $t$, then:
$$
\ket{\psi(t + 1)} = U\ket{\psi(t)}  
$$

Now, consider a rule $\mathfrak{U}$, that is associated with each instant of time $t_{0}, t_{1}, t_{2},\dots, t_{n-1}$, such that our unitary matrices are $\mathfrak{U}[t_{0}], \mathfrak{U}[t_{1}], \mathfrak{U}[t_{2}], \dots, \mathfrak{U}[t_{n-1}]$. Then if our initial state vector we can obtain the **orbit** of it by:
$$
\mathfrak{U}[t_{0}]\ket{\psi} \to \mathfrak{U}[t_{1}]\mathfrak{U}[t_{0}]\ket{\psi} \to \mathfrak{U}[t_{2}]\mathfrak{U}[t_{1}]\mathfrak{U}[t_{0}]\ket{\psi} \to \cdots \to \mathfrak{U}[t_{n-1}] \cdots \mathfrak{U}[t_{2}]\mathfrak{U}[t_{1}]\mathfrak{U}[t_{0}]\ket{\psi}
$$

It is called the orbit because it can be **reversed** using the adjoint of the unitary operator, thus:
$$
\mathfrak{U}[t_{0}]\ket{\psi} \leftarrow  \mathfrak{U}[t_{1}]^{\dagger}\mathfrak{U}[t_{1}]\mathfrak{U}[t_{0}]\ket{\psi}
$$

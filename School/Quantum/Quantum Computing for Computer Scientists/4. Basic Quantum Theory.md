## 4.1 Quantum States
Through the XIX Century there was a discussion whether if light was solely waves and matter was solely made out of particles. Using experiments such as the **diffraction experiment** and the **photoelectric experiment**, scientists found out that both matter and light have particle and wave-like behavior.

Let's say we want to know the probability of a subatomic particle to land on one of the many points on a line where it can land. For instance, each one of them can be expressed as the following:
$$
\begin{matrix}
\ket{x_{1}} \to 
\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 0 & \cdots & 0
\end{bmatrix} \\
\ket{x_{2}} \to 
\begin{bmatrix}
0 & 1 & \cdots & 0
\end{bmatrix} \\
\vdots \\
\ket{x_{n}} \to 
\begin{bmatrix}
0 & 0 & \cdots & 1
\end{bmatrix}
\end{matrix}
$$

As we have seen, initially the particle is in a **fuzzy** state that we don't really know, which is a **superposition** of all its possible states, that can be represented as:
$$
\ket{\psi} = c_{1}\ket{x_{1}} + c_{2}\ket{x_{2}} + \cdots + c_{n}\ket{x_{n}}
$$

We say that each $c_{i}$ is a **complex amplitude**, and this combination is useful for obtaining the probability of the particle of landing in a given state, obtained from:
$$
p(x_{i}) = \frac{\left| c_{i} \right|^{2}}{\left| \ket{\psi}  \right|^{2}} = \frac{\left| c_{i} \right|^{2}}{\sum_{j = 1}^{n}\left| c_{j} \right|^{2}}
$$

Two **complex states** $\ket{\psi_{1}}$ and $\ket{\psi_{2}}$ are the same if there exists a $c \in \mathbb{C}$ such that $\ket{\psi_{1}} = c\ket{\psi_{2}}$. This is because:
$$
p(x_{i}) = \frac{\left| c_{1i} \right|^{2}}{\left| \ket{\psi_{1}}  \right|^{2}} = \frac{\left| cc_{1i} \right|^{2}}{\left| \ket{\psi_{1}}  \right|^{2}}
$$

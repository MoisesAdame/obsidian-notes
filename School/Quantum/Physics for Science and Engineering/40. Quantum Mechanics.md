## 40.1 The Wavefunction
### Introduction,  Making Sense
In order to understand the wavefunction, we need to find a relation between particles and waves. This can be done considering the following. First, we know that the probability ($P$) per unit volume ($V$)  of **finding a photon** is proportional to the number of photons ($n_{photons}$) per unit volume ($V$):
$$
\frac{P}{V} \propto \frac{n_{photons}}{V}
$$

Consequently, the number of photons ($n_{photons}$) per unit volume ($V$) is proportional to the wave intensity ($I$) (a value that can be understood as the average in time of the Poynting vector $\vec{S} = \frac{1}{\mu_{0}} \vec{E} \times \vec{B}$), thus:
$$
I = \frac{E^{2}}{2c\mu_{0}}, ~ I \propto E^{2}
$$
$$
\frac{n_{photons}}{V} \propto I \propto E^{2}
$$

This means that there is a wave-particle duality of the electromagnetic radiation, consequently the wave amplitude associated to a given particle is called **wavefunction ($\Psi$)**. This function is dependent on all the particles in the system as well as the time, written as:
$$
\Psi(\vec{r_{1}}, \vec{r_{2}}, \vec{r_{3}}, \dots, \vec{r_{j}}, \dots, t) = \psi(\vec{r_{j}})e^{-\omega it}
$$

### Probabilities
The probability of finding a particle per unit volume is proportional to the **modulus squared** of the wave function:
$$
\left| \psi \right|^{2} = \psi^{*}\psi
$$

This quantity is also known as the **probability density**. Considering this, the probability of finding a particle per unit volume is equal to the probability density per unit volume:
$$
\frac{P(x, y, z)}{dV} = \frac{\left| \psi \right|^{2}}{dV} \implies P(x, y, z)dV = \left| \psi \right|^{2}dV
$$

### De Broglie's Wave Length
De Broglie, found out that the proton's wave length can be specified by the quantity of movement it has, thus:
$$
\lambda = \frac{h}{p} = 
\frac{h}{m \dot{x}} =
\begin{bmatrix}
\frac{m^{2}kg / s}{kg \cdot m/s}
\end{bmatrix} =
\begin{bmatrix}
m
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Now, assume that we know the momentum in the x axis of a particle, then it's wavelength is $\lambda = \frac{\hbar}{p_{x}}$, and considering that it is equally probable to be anywhere over x, the wavefunction of this particle can be written as:
$$
\psi(x) = A(\cos(kx) + i\sin(kx)) = Ae^{ixk}, ~ k = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}
$$

### Probability of Finding a Particle
Considering the previously talked about formula for the probability of finding a particle in a given volume, by integrating both sides we know:
$$
P(a \leq x \leq b) = \int_{a}^{b} \left| \psi \right|^{2} dx
$$

For instance, we know that the wavefunction is **normalized** if, that is to say, the particle exists somewhere in space:
$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \left| \psi \right|^{2} = 1
$$

Now, consider that we want to know the most common result of an experiment, this can be done using what is known as the **expectation value**:
$$
\braket{ f(x) } = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\psi^{*} f(x) \psi dx 
$$

## 40.2 Model Analysis: Quantum Particle Under Border Conditions
### A Particle Inside a Box
![[fig40.2.1.gif|300]]

Consider the system shown in the section A. We'd like to find a **wave function** $\psi(x)$, such that $\forall x \leq 0 ~\wedge~ \forall x \geq L, ~ \psi(x) =0$ and also it has to be continuous.

Additionally, we can define the **potential energy** inside te box, non-dependent of the position, being $0$ anywhere inside and $\infty$ outside, thus:
$$
\psi(x) = A\sin\left( \frac{2\pi x}{\lambda} \right), ~ \lambda=\frac{h}{p} ~ \text{(De Broglie's Wave len.)}
$$

The previous means that $\psi(0) = \psi(L) = 0$, which ends up as follows:
$$
\psi(0) = A\sin(0) = 0, ~ \psi(L) = A\sin\left( \frac{2\pi L}{\lambda} \right) = 0
$$
The latter can only be managed letting:
$$
\pi n = \frac{2\pi L}{\lambda} \implies \lambda = \frac{2L}{n}
$$
This means that for each **wave length**, there is a **quantum state**, thus:
$$
\psi(x) = A\sin\left( \frac{2L}{n} \right), n \in N - \{ 0 \}
$$

After **normalizing** the wave function we get:
$$
\psi_{n}(x) = \sqrt{ \frac{2}{L} }\sin\left( \frac{2L}{n} \right)
$$
Now, considering the **Law of Conservation of Energy** we know that $E = K + U$, and as we explained, potential energy is cero, and we can obtain kinetic energy as follows:
$$
E = \frac{1}{2}m u^{2} + 0
$$
Now, considering De Broglie's **wave-length formula**, we see that:
$$
p = \frac{h}{\lambda} = m u \implies u = \frac{h}{\lambda m}
$$
Ending up with:
$$
E_{n} = \frac{h^{2}}{2\lambda^{2}m} = \frac{h^{2}}{8L^{2}m}n^{2}
$$
